Special Management cases will usually include that class of cases commonly referred to as "complex
litigation" as that term has been used in the Manuals for Complex Litigation. The first manual was prepared
in 1969 and the Manual for Complex Litigation Second, MCL 2d was prepared in 1985. This term is
intended to include cases that present unusual problems and require extraordinary treatment. See §0.1 of the
first manual. Cases may require special or intense management by the court due to one or more of the
following factors: (1) large number of parties; (2) large number of claims or defenses; (3) complex factual
issues; (4) large volume of evidence; (5) problems locating or preserving evidence; (6) extensive discovery;
(7) exceptionally long time needed to prepare for disposition; (8) decision needed within an exceptionally
short time; and (9) need to decide preliminary issues before final disposition. It may include two or more
related cases. Complex litigation typically includes such cases as antitrust cases; cases involving a large
number of parties or an unincorporated association of large membership; cases involving requests for
injunctive relief affecting the operation of large business entities; patent cases; copyright and trademark
cases; common disaster cases such as those arising from aircraft crashes or marine disasters; actions brought
by individual stockholders; stockholder's derivative and stockholder's representative actions; class actions or
potential class actions; and other civil (and criminal) cases involving unusual multiplicity or complexity of
factual issues. See §0.22 of the first Manual for Complex Litigation and Manual for Complex Litigation
Second, Chapter 33.

on behalf of the Plaintiff, Antoinette Colon (“Plaintiff Colon”), a former employee of the
Defendant, Educational Commission for Foreign Medical Graduates (“Defendant”), who
has been harmed by the Defendant’s discriminatory and retaliatory employment
practices.

§12101, et seq. (“ADA”), the Family and Medical Leave Act (“FMLA”), 29 U.S.C.
§2601 et seq., and the Pennsylvania Human Relations Act (“PHRA”), 43 P.S. §951 et

seq.

IV. STATEMENT OF CLAIMS:

11. Plaintiff Colon was employed by the Defendant from on or about February
10, 2014 until on or about May 23, 2017, the date of her unlawful termination.

12. During the course of her employment with the Defendant, Plaintiff Colon
held the position of Benefits Administrator, and at all times maintained a satisfactory job
performance rating in said capacity.

13. By way of background, from in or about December of 2016 until the date of
her termination, Plaintiff Colon reported directly to Kiandra Johnson (“Johnson”),
Benefits and Compensation Manager.

14. Onor about January 27, 2017, Plaintiff Colon was diagnosed with
Depression. Said medical condition constitutes a disability within the meaning of the
Americans with Disabilities Act (“ADA”) and the Pennsylvania Human Relations Act
(“PHRA”), in that it substantially impairs one or more of her major life activities,
including, but not limited to, sleeping.

15. Onthe same date, at the recommendation of her physician, Plaintiff Colon
requested a brief medical leave of absence under the Family and Medical Leave Act
(“FMLA”) as a reasonable accommodation for her disability. The Defendant granted said
request.

16. Onor about April 20, 2017, Betty Lehew (“Lehew”), Vice President of
Human Resources, advised Plaintiff Colon that her FMLA leave had expired and inquired

about her return to work. Plaintiff Colon informed Lehew that she intended to visit her
physician the following day for evaluation.

17. Onor about April 21, 2017, Plaintiff Colon’s physician released her to
return to work without restrictions and, on or about April 25, 2017, Plaintiff Colon
returned to work. Thereafter, the Defendant, through its agents, servants, and employees,
began subjecting Plaintiff Colon to discrimination based on her actual and/or perceived
disability and/or record of impairment and retaliation for requesting a reasonable
accommodation for her disability and exercising her rights pursuant to the FMLA.

18. | By way of example, on the date Plaintiff Colon returned to work, Lehew
and Katie Chilsom (“Chilsom”’), Human Resources Generalist, summoned Plaintiff Colon
to a meeting and questioned whether Plaintiff Colon agreed with her physician’s decision
to release her to return to work. Plaintiff Colon stated that she agreed with said decision
and that she was able and willing to perform her job responsibilities.

19. Approximately thirty (30) minutes thereafter, Lehew and Johnson requested
Plaintiff Colon’s presence at another meeting. During said meeting, Johnson accused
Plaintiff Colon of expressing that she was unhappy with her job prior to her leave of
absence and insinuated that she was not able to handle the responsibilities of her position.
Furthermore, Johnson issued to Plaintiff Colon an unjustified “Corrective Action.”

20.  Inresponse thereto, Plaintiff Colon expressed her belief that the issuance of
the “Corrective Action” was unjustified and cited to specific inaccuracies within the
document. Moreover, said “Corrective Action” referenced various “incidents” which had

occurred over six (6) months prior.

22. Significantly, the Defendant also terminated the employment of Robert

23. Plaintiff Colon believes and avers that the Defendant subjected her to
